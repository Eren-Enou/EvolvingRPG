# First hours and emerging character journeys

Proposal, not implemented. Related: [design atlas](GROWTH_DESIGN_ATLAS.md), [mission design](MISSION_AND_ENCOUNTER_DESIGN.md), [mastery and specialization](MASTERY_AND_SPECIALIZATION_DESIGN.md). Names and timing ranges are placeholders; the opening must be playtested with the actual controls and final readability.

## Start with a reason to move

Candidate premise: a small expedition outpost is reopening a route through an abandoned relay site. Patrols are returning with reports of familiar creatures behaving differently around the ruins. The player is a newly arrived fighter, not a predeclared savior or a secretly complete champion.

This premise supports repeatable short expeditions, an evolving local threat, a hub that visibly improves, and character discovery without explaining the entire world in the opening minute. It is a narrative wrapper to compare, not approved lore. The game can keep mystery without hiding basic rules.

The opening question is concrete: "Can I make it through the route and bring back a reliable account of what is happening?" The longer question is personal: "Which kind of fighter am I becoming while doing this?"

## A candidate first session

Times are observation ranges, never forced pacing gates. A skilled returning player should not spend twenty minutes repeating a tutorial. A slower player should not lose a discovery because a timer passed.

| Beat | Candidate duration | Player experience | System taught | Stop/revise if |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arrive and orient | 2–4 min | Walk from arrival point to a visible expedition board through an open court | Movement, camera, interact; one destination | The player needs repeated map arrows to cross a tiny hub |
| Safe action introduction | 3–6 min, optional for repeat players | Test Light, Heavy, jump and directional dodge in an adjacent practice space | Distinct commitments, visible hit feedback, no mastery grinding | Tutorials require exact mandatory attack chains |
| First field exchange | 4–7 min | Fight one readable opponent with space to retreat | Enemy commitment, recovery, positioning | The only viable response is Heavy spam or a hidden stat advantage |
| Route decision | 3–5 min | Choose open yard or sheltered passage to reach the same relay | Geometry and encounter approach | One route is an undisclosed permanent progression trap |
| Culminating exchange | 4–8 min | Meet a guarded role with a familiar threat nearby, initially separated | Different openings; target priority | Mixed pressure obscures the newly learned rules |
| Return and reflect | 3–5 min | See a concise account of practice and choose a first development if eligible | Practice versus choice; future possibilities | The report awards a build automatically or requires reading every log |

Expected exploratory session range: roughly 20–35 minutes, with skips, retries and reading producing wider variation. Do not tune rewards to force that duration. First meaningful investment should follow enough experience to understand the alternative, not an arbitrary playtime requirement.

## Hub functions before hub scale

Build one visible loop around four functions: mission board, practice space, character-development point, and return point. They can initially be graybox stations with labels. Do not build a large city to connect four menus.

The mission board explains objective, broad known danger, expected commitment, retry policy, and reward types. The practice space provides consequence-free rehearsal without durable progression from idle dummy hits. The development point presents current options and tradeoffs. The return point summarizes what actually happened and what was secured.

A future mentor can contextualize discoveries, but must not be the only way to decode a broken rule. The journal retains explanations after dialogue is skipped. Mission selection and character development should be reachable without long repeated walks once the location is understood.

First proposed hub change: restoring the relay adds a readable regional report or opens a second contract. This is evidence of the world's response, not a hidden player-power bonus. Avoid immediately creating crafting, housing, multiple reputation factions and vendor currencies.

## Teach contrast, not rotation

Introduce Light as a quick decision that can fit an opening. Introduce Heavy as a deliberate commitment that may destabilize an opponent. Let the player see a Heavy punish a long recovery or break guard; do not manufacture an enemy immune to Light to force compliance.

Introduce Dodge as movement through space. A near enemy strike makes distance and direction understandable; a giant on-screen "successful dodge" badge is dishonest until authoritative evidence exists. Space remains jump. Jump need not become a damage rotation or a required defense in the opening.

Let ordinary movement and Q/E strafing solve at least one threat without spending stamina. An active defense economy should encourage preparation and positioning, not demand continuous dodge use. Current permissive stamina remains unchanged until its separately planned balance experiment.

Optional guidance should point out observed facts: "That strike stayed committed to its direction" or "The guard broke." It should not label the player's personality or declare that frequent Heavy use has made them a Breaker.

## The first choice

After the player has used ordinary Heavy in a real exchange, present two candidates with a reversible preview: planted disruption and committed advancement. Show the exact changed behavior, credible drawback, resource cost, and whether the choice is a trial or an actual investment.

The comparison can use a training opponent without granting practice. Previewing is not owning. The first game-shaped prototype can supply a controlled investment token as a mission milestone; the eventual earning cadence remains open. A player who declines the purchase should still have viable ordinary combat and a clear next mission.

Never attach the first choice to a misleading "recommended" path inferred from a few attacks. A plain observation is useful: "You broke two guards." A forced recommendation is not: "The system has chosen your class."

## Two possible Warrior histories

### Warrior A: seeks reliable disruption

The player prefers to hold position long enough to break frontal guard. They invest in the planted Heavy, learn to prepare its longer commitment, and sometimes choose a quick Light or retreat rather than force another Heavy. A new enemy with a narrow punish window exposes their weakness without invalidating the build.

Later, a guard-break follow-up might be revealed from relevant experience. The player can decline it in favor of a defensive supporting technique. Their identity is visible in how they create openings, not a required named subclass. Their main drawback remains limited movement while committing.

### Warrior B: seeks movement into retaliation

The player likes drawing a committed strike out of line, then advancing back into contact. They invest in the advancing Heavy. Actual evasive retaliation may reveal a related possibility once verified defensive evidence exists. They still risk overshooting, wall collision and extra recovery on misses.

A guarded enemy may take more preparation than it does for Warrior A. That comparison should be a different problem with a viable solution, not an insurmountable stat wall. Basic Light, Heavy, movement and ordinary guard pressure remain available.

Both characters learn from the same region. Neither receives a hidden class assignment. The account may remember a discovered possibility after one character finds it, but the other must fulfill its own prerequisites and investment requirements.

## A candidate next two sessions

**Session two: test the first choice.** Return to the same site with a changed objective or composition. Reuse familiar geometry so the player can recognize how their approach differs. Add one new question, such as a mobile threat behind a guarded front line. Let the new technique sometimes be the wrong choice in a specific moment without making the entire specialization wrong.

**Session three: pursue an interest.** Offer two contracts with truthful descriptions: one emphasizes holding a contested approach, the other tracking an exposed courier through open ground. Neither contract guarantees a hidden talent or permanently gates a branch. Let both supply normal progress and opportunities for the selected discipline.

The player can pursue clues, equipment needs, mission completion or a preferred fight. A discovery should feel like a meaningful consequence of play rather than the only valid objective. Do not add daily time gates to make the hub feel busy.

## Failure, retreat and return

Initial proposal: character knowledge and committed investments survive defeat. A failed encounter cannot be the first time the player discovers that a permanent talent was at risk. Unsecured mission loot and the completion reward may be lost or reduced, with the policy disclosed before departure.

For a small prototype, prefer a clearly defined checkpoint retry over recovery of a dropped corpse inventory. Replayable encounter rewards must not duplicate. Character practice retained after failure must consume encounter budgets even if enemies reset, or defeat becomes a farming tool. Persistence and reward rules must exist before making these durable.

Voluntary extraction should be a visible option at an agreed safe point, not a menu that teleports the character out of active damage. Candidate comparison: safe-site extraction versus immediate abandon with loss of unsecured rewards. Avoid forcing a player who has ten minutes left into an uncertain hour-long run.

After failure, the report should distinguish: what was learned, what was secured, what remains incomplete, and what can be retried. Offer the build view as an option, not a mandatory optimization lecture. Retry friction should not dominate the time spent learning an encounter.

## Accessibility and information pacing

Use shape, motion and sound as well as color for windup, active threat and recovery. Scale text, allow camera sensitivity/zoom adjustment and preserve remapping as an eventual input requirement. Do not attach progression eligibility to default physical keys.

Teach one new idea per encounter beat. Hide advanced diagnostics by default and keep them available for investigation. Let the player inspect changed action timing in the hub without requiring frame counting during combat. Discovery notifications wait for a safe moment or become a small non-blocking entry.

Do not require a player to perform avoidable high-risk maneuvers for all basic growth. A dodge-specific discovery can be optional; core Warrior viability cannot depend on perfect evasions. Future accessibility options should be assessed for how their actual outcomes map to evidence, rather than silently disabling progression for remapped or assisted input.

## Future evaluation notes

Observe whether a new player can identify an opening, describe Light versus Heavy, choose a route, explain the first investment's cost, and find the next mission. Record confusion and unexpected behavior before explaining the intended solution.

Compare a first-time player with an informed repeat player. The repeat player should reach meaningful decisions faster without inheriting character power. If most of the opening's interest disappears on replay, shorten required exposition and let choices create the variation.

No opening content, hub, mission rewards or failure system described here has been implemented. Player testing is required before turning any pacing or policy into a requirement.
